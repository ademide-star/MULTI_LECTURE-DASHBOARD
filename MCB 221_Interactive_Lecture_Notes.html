MODULE 01

Introduction and History of Microbiology

Definition & scope · Discovery of microorganisms · Germ theory · The Golden Age

Learning Outcomes

  • Define microbiology and outline its major branches
  • Describe the discovery of microorganisms by van Leeuwenhoek
  • Explain the spontaneous generation controversy and its resolution
  • State Koch's postulates and their significance

1.1 Definition & Scope

Microbiology is the scientific study of microscopic organisms — bacteria, viruses, fungi, protozoa, and microscopic algae. The name derives from Greek: mikros (small), bios (life), logos (study).

BranchFocus
BacteriologyStudy of bacteria
VirologyStudy of viruses
MycologyStudy of fungi
ProtozoologyStudy of protozoa
PhycologyStudy of algae
ParasitologyStudy of parasites

Applied microbiology spans medical, industrial, agricultural, food, environmental and pharmaceutical microbiology — underpinning disease prevention, antibiotic/vaccine development, food production, biotechnology, and bioremediation.

1.2 Discovery of Microorganisms & the Spontaneous Generation Controversy

Antonie van Leeuwenhoek (1632–1723), a Dutch cloth merchant, built simple microscopes (up to 300× magnification) and in 1676 became the first to observe bacteria, which he called "animalcules." He is regarded as the Father of Microbiology.

The theory of spontaneous generation — that life could arise from non-living matter — was challenged step by step: Francesco Redi (1668) showed maggots came from fly eggs, not rotting meat; John Needham's boiled-broth flasks still grew microbes (insufficient heating); Lazzaro Spallanzani sealed and boiled flasks longer, preventing growth; and Louis Pasteur (1861) delivered the definitive disproof with his swan-neck flask experiment — air could enter the curved neck but dust/microbes settled in the bend, so broth stayed sterile until tilted to contact the trapped dust.

🧪 Pasteur's Other Contributions
  • Developed pasteurization; saved the French wine and silk industries
  • Developed vaccines for rabies and anthrax
  • Established the Germ Theory of Disease
  • Discovered anaerobic life (fermentation without oxygen)

1.3 Germ Theory & Koch's Postulates

Ignaz Semmelweis cut maternal mortality by requiring doctors to wash hands in chlorine solution; Joseph Lister introduced antiseptic surgery using carbolic acid (phenol), becoming the Father of Antiseptic Surgery. Robert Koch put germ theory on a rigorous scientific footing.

🔬 Koch's Postulates
  • The microorganism must be present in every case of the disease
  • It must be isolated and grown in pure culture
  • The culture must cause disease when introduced into a healthy host
  • The same organism must be re-isolated from that experimentally infected host

Koch identified Mycobacterium tuberculosis (1882) and Vibrio cholerae (1883), pioneered solid-media culture using agar, developed bacterial staining techniques, and — with assistant Julius Petri — the Petri dish. He won the 1905 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. The Golden Age of Microbiology (1857–1914) that followed saw germ theory established, pure-culture technique perfected, and most major pathogens discovered.

MODULE 02

Classification of Organisms

Taxonomic hierarchy · Three-domain system · Binomial nomenclature

Learning Outcomes

  • Describe the taxonomic hierarchy from domain to species
  • Explain the three-domain system of life
  • Differentiate prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells
  • Apply binomial nomenclature correctly

2.1 Taxonomic Hierarchy

Classification organizes organisms hierarchically, from broadest to most specific: Domain → Kingdom → Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species. Each rank groups organisms by shared characteristics, with species — a group of organisms capable of interbreeding and sharing a high degree of similarity — as the fundamental, most specific unit.

DomainCell TypeExamples
BacteriaProkaryoticMost disease-causing and environmental bacteria
ArchaeaProkaryotic, distinct biochemistryExtremophiles — methanogens, halophiles, thermophiles
EukaryaEukaryoticFungi, protozoa, algae, plants, animals

2.2 Prokaryotes vs Eukaryotes & Nomenclature

Prokaryotic cells (bacteria, archaea) lack a membrane-bound nucleus and most organelles — their DNA lies free in the nucleoid. Eukaryotic cells (fungi, protozoa, algae, plants, animals) have a true membrane-enclosed nucleus and organelles (mitochondria, ER, Golgi).

📛 Binomial Nomenclature
  • Every species gets a two-part Latinized name: Genus species
  • Genus is capitalized; species epithet is lowercase; both italicized (or underlined if handwritten)
  • Example: Escherichia coli — genus Escherichia, species coli
  • After first use, genus may be abbreviated: E. coli

MODULE 03

Bacterial Structure and Morphology

External structures · Cell wall (Gram +/−) · Endospores · Shapes & colonial morphology

Learning Outcomes

  • Describe bacterial external structures and their functions
  • Differentiate Gram-positive and Gram-negative cell walls
  • Describe endospore structure and its survival advantage
  • Identify bacterial shapes, arrangements and colonial morphology descriptors

3.1 External Structures

The glycocalyx (capsule if organized/firmly attached, slime layer if loose) protects against phagocytosis and desiccation, aids surface attachment/biofilm formation, and shields against antibiotics — essential to virulence in Streptococcus pneumoniae. Flagella (protein flagellin; filament + hook + basal body) drive motility and are arranged monotrichous (one pole), lophotrichous (tuft at one pole), amphitrichous (both poles), or peritrichous (all around). Fimbriae are short adhesion appendages (hundreds per cell, non-motile); pili are longer, fewer, and include sex pili (conjugation) and type IV pili (twitching motility).

3.2 Cell Wall: Gram-Positive vs Gram-Negative

FeatureGram-PositiveGram-Negative
PeptidoglycanThick (20–80 nm), multilayeredThin (2–3 nm), single layer
Outer membraneAbsentPresent, contains LPS
Teichoic acidsPresentAbsent
Gram stain resultRetains crystal violet — purpleTakes safranin — pink/red

Peptidoglycan is built from alternating N-acetylglucosamine (NAG) and N-acetylmuramic acid (NAM) sugars cross-linked by peptide bridges — the target of penicillin and vancomycin. Gram-negative LPS (endotoxin) has three parts: toxic Lipid A, a core polysaccharide, and the antigenic O-antigen; it triggers fever and septic shock. Atypical walls include Mycobacteria (waxy mycolic acid, acid-fast) and Mycoplasma (no cell wall at all, sterols in the membrane instead).

3.3 Endospores & Bacterial Shapes

Endospores are dormant, highly resistant structures formed by genera like Bacillus and Clostridium under adverse conditions (nutrient starvation), surviving heat, radiation, desiccation and chemicals for years. Structure: a DNA/ribosome-containing core, cortex (loosely cross-linked peptidoglycan), and a tough keratin-like spore coat.

ShapeArrangement TermsExample
Cocci (spherical)Diplococci, streptococci, staphylococci, tetrads, sarcinaeStaphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pyogenes
Bacilli (rods)Diplobacilli, streptobacilli, coccobacilliEscherichia coli, Bacillus anthracis
Spiral formsVibrios (comma), spirilla (rigid), spirochetes (flexible coil)Vibrio cholerae, Treponema pallidum

Colonial morphology on solid media is described by form, elevation, margin, surface, pigmentation, opacity, size, and haemolysis pattern (α-partial/green, β-complete/clear, γ-none) — used for preliminary identification and contamination checks.

🧪 Practical Manual — Bacterial Morphology & Motility
Duration: ~3 hoursWorks in pairs/small groups

Objective

Observe bacterial cell shape and arrangement by simple/wet-mount microscopy, determine motility by the hanging-drop method, and describe colonial morphology from a streak plate.

Materials

  • Broth and agar-slant cultures of two or more bacteria (e.g., E. coli, Staphylococcus aureus, Bacillus subtilis)
  • Compound microscope, immersion oil, glass slides, cavity slides, coverslips
  • Inoculating loop, Bunsen burner, methylene blue stain
  • Sterile nutrient agar plates

Procedure

  1. Prepare a simple stain: smear a loopful of broth culture on a slide, air-dry, heat-fix, flood with methylene blue for 1 minute, rinse and blot dry.
  2. Examine under oil immersion (100×) and record cell shape and arrangement for each organism.
  3. For motility, place a small drop of broth culture on a coverslip; invert a cavity slide over it so the drop hangs in the well (hanging-drop method); examine under high power for true swimming movement versus passive Brownian motion.
  4. Using a sterile loop, streak one organism onto a nutrient agar plate using the quadrant streak technique to obtain isolated colonies; incubate at 37°C for 18–24 hours.
  5. Examine the resulting colonies and describe form, elevation, margin, surface, pigmentation and size.

Expected Result / Interpretation

Cocci appear round in clusters or chains depending on species; bacilli appear as rods, singly or in chains. Truly motile organisms (e.g., E. coli) show directional movement in the hanging drop; non-motile organisms show only Brownian jiggling in place. Colony descriptors are recorded in standard terminology for later identification work.

⚠️ Safety: Treat all cultures as potentially pathogenic. Work near a flame for aseptic technique, dispose of slides/loops in disinfectant, and decontaminate the bench before and after the session.

MODULE 04

Fungal Structure and Morphology

Yeasts vs moulds · Hyphal types · Reproduction · Spores

Learning Outcomes

  • Differentiate yeasts and moulds
  • Describe hyphal structure and types
  • Outline asexual and sexual fungal reproduction
  • Identify fungi by microscopic examination

4.1 Fungal Forms

Fungi are eukaryotic, chitin-walled organisms. Yeasts are unicellular, oval/round, and reproduce mainly by budding. Moulds are multicellular/filamentous, made of thread-like hyphae that together form a mycelium. Dimorphic fungi (e.g., Histoplasma, Candida albicans in some contexts) switch between yeast and mould forms depending on temperature — mould at room temperature, yeast at body temperature.

Hyphal TypeDescription
SeptateCross-walls (septa) divide the hypha into cell-like compartments
Aseptate (coenocytic)No septa — a continuous multinucleate cytoplasm
Vegetative hyphaeGrow into the substrate, absorb nutrients
Aerial/reproductive hyphaeGrow upward, bear spores

4.2 Reproduction & Spores

Asexual reproduction dominates in the lab and includes budding (yeasts), fission, fragmentation, and spore formation — conidia (external, unenclosed asexual spores, e.g., Aspergillus, Penicillium) and sporangiospores (formed inside a sporangium, e.g., Rhizopus). Sexual reproduction involves fusion of compatible mating types, producing sexual spores (ascospores, basidiospores, zygospores) — the basis for classifying fungi into groups such as Ascomycota, Basidiomycota, and Zygomycota.

🍄 Clinically/Industrially Important Genera
  • Candida — yeast infections (candidiasis)
  • Aspergillus — aspergillosis, mycotoxin producer, also used industrially
  • Saccharomyces — brewing, baking (non-pathogenic)
  • Rhizopus — bread mould, mucormycosis in immunocompromised hosts
  • Trichophyton — dermatophyte, ringworm/athlete's foot

🧪 Practical Manual — Microscopic Examination of Fungi
Duration: ~3 hoursWorks in pairs/small groups

Objective

Prepare wet mounts of fungal cultures and identify yeast and mould structures — hyphae, septa, spores — using lactophenol cotton blue staining.

Materials

  • Pure mould cultures on Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (e.g., Rhizopus, Aspergillus, Penicillium), and a yeast culture (e.g., Saccharomyces or Candida)
  • Lactophenol cotton blue stain, glass slides, coverslips, mounted needle/forceps
  • Compound microscope

Procedure

  1. Place a drop of lactophenol cotton blue on a clean slide.
  2. Using a sterile mounted needle, tease a small portion of the mould colony (including some aerial hyphae bearing spores) and transfer it into the stain drop.
  3. Gently spread the sample and cover with a coverslip, avoiding air bubbles.
  4. Examine first under low power (10×) to locate hyphae, then high power (40×) to observe septation and spore-bearing structures.
  5. For the yeast, prepare a simple wet mount in a drop of water or stain and observe budding cells under oil immersion.

Expected Result / Interpretation

Moulds show a branching network of hyphae with visible septa (septate) or none (aseptate/coenocytic), and characteristic spore-bearing structures (e.g., the black sporangium of Rhizopus, the brush-like conidiophore of Penicillium). Yeasts appear as oval cells, often showing budding daughter cells attached to a parent cell.

⚠️ Safety: Mould spores are easily aerosolized — open plates and prepare mounts inside a biosafety cabinet or with minimal agitation, and never sniff a fungal culture plate.

MODULE 05

Viral Structure

General properties · Capsid & envelope · Symmetry classes · Living vs non-living debate

Learning Outcomes

  • Describe the general properties of viruses
  • Identify the basic components of a virion
  • Differentiate helical, icosahedral and complex symmetry
  • Explain why enveloped and non-enveloped viruses differ in environmental resistance

5.1 General Characteristics

Viruses are obligate intracellular parasites — nucleic acid surrounded by a protein coat, unable to replicate independently since they lack ribosomes and metabolic machinery. They contain either DNA or RNA (never both), range 20–300 nm, and are filterable through bacterial filters. Their "living vs non-living" status is debated: they carry genetic material, reproduce (inside a host), and evolve — but have no cellular structure, no metabolism, cannot reproduce alone, and can even be crystallized like a chemical compound.

5.2 Structure of a Virion

ComponentDescription
Nucleic acid coreDNA or RNA; ss/ds, linear/circular, segmented/non-segmented
CapsidProtein coat of repeating capsomere subunits; protects genome, determines shape, is antigenic
NucleocapsidNucleic acid + capsid together — the complete particle without an envelope
EnvelopeLipid bilayer from the host membrane, found in some viruses; carries glycoprotein spikes
Spikes (peplomers)Glycoprotein projections for attachment/fusion — e.g., influenza haemagglutinin & neuraminidase

Some virions also carry their own enzymes — reverse transcriptase in retroviruses, lysozyme in bacteriophages.

5.3 Capsid Symmetry & Envelope Sensitivity

🧬 Three Symmetry Classes
  • Helical — rod/filamentous, capsomeres spiral around the nucleic acid (tobacco mosaic virus, rabies, Ebola, influenza)
  • Icosahedral — 20 triangular faces, 12 vertices, roughly spherical, the most efficient way to enclose space (adenovirus, poliovirus, herpesvirus)
  • Complex — combines elements, e.g., T4 bacteriophage's icosahedral head + helical tail + tail fibres, or the brick-shaped poxviruses

Enveloped viruses (influenza, HIV, herpes simplex, SARS-CoV-2) are fragile — destroyed by drying, detergents, heat and alcohol — so they spread mainly via close contact/body fluids. Non-enveloped (naked) viruses (poliovirus, adenovirus, norovirus, hepatitis A) resist drying, detergents and even stomach acid, so they spread readily via fomites, food and water.

MODULE 06

Microbial Nutrition and Metabolism

Nutrient classes · Nutritional/oxygen groups · Transport · Culture media · Growth curve

Learning Outcomes

  • Classify microbes by carbon and energy source
  • Classify microbes by oxygen requirement
  • Describe nutrient uptake mechanisms across the membrane
  • Describe the bacterial growth curve and methods of measuring growth

6.1 Nutritional Classification

Essential macronutrients (C, N, H, O, P, S) build organic molecules, nucleic acids and ATP; micronutrients (Fe, Mg, K, Ca, Zn, Mn, Cu, Co, Mo) act as enzyme cofactors; growth factors (vitamins, amino acids, purines/pyrimidines) are organic compounds a cell cannot synthesize itself.

Nutritional TypeCarbon SourceEnergy SourceExamples
PhotoautotrophCO₂LightCyanobacteria, algae, purple/green sulfur bacteria
ChemoautotrophCO₂Inorganic chemicalsNitrifying, sulfur, iron bacteria
PhotoheterotrophOrganic compoundsLightPurple/green non-sulfur bacteria
ChemoheterotrophOrganic compoundsOrganic chemicalsMost bacteria, fungi, protozoa (saprophytes, parasites)

6.2 Oxygen Requirements & Nutrient Uptake

GroupO₂ RelationshipExample
Obligate aerobesRequire O₂; have catalase & superoxide dismutaseMycobacterium tuberculosis
Obligate anaerobesO₂ is toxic; lack protective enzymesClostridium
Facultative anaerobesGrow with or without O₂, prefer aerobicE. coli
MicroaerophilesNeed low O₂ (2–10%); normal air is toxicHelicobacter pylori
Aerotolerant anaerobesFerment regardless of O₂, but tolerate itStreptococcus pyogenes

Nutrients cross the membrane by simple diffusion (small molecules, high→low concentration), facilitated diffusion (via carrier/permease proteins, still down the gradient), and active transport (against the gradient, using ATP directly — primary, e.g., ABC transporters — or indirectly via the proton motive force — secondary, via uniporters/symporters/antiporters).

6.3 The Bacterial Growth Curve

Growth proceeds by binary fission (DNA replication → elongation → septum formation → separation). E. coli doubles roughly every 20 minutes under optimal conditions; M. tuberculosis takes 15–20 hours. Population growth follows Nt = N₀ × 2ⁿ.

📈 Four Phases
  • Lag phase — adapting, synthesizing enzymes, no increase in cell number
  • Log (exponential) phase — rapid constant division, most metabolically active, most antibiotic-susceptible
  • Stationary phase — growth rate = death rate; nutrient depletion; secondary metabolites (antibiotics) often produced here
  • Death (decline) phase — death rate exceeds growth rate; nutrient exhaustion, toxic waste, cell lysis

Growth is measured directly by plate count (CFU/ml), direct microscopic count, or membrane filtration; and indirectly by turbidity (OD₆₀₀), metabolic activity, or dry weight.

🧪 Practical Manual — Culture Media Preparation & Isolation Techniques
Duration: ~3 hoursWorks in pairs/small groups

Objective

Prepare a solid culture medium and practise the streak-plate, pour-plate and spread-plate techniques for isolating and enumerating bacteria.

Materials

  • Nutrient agar powder, distilled water, conical flask, weighing balance
  • Autoclave, Petri dishes, inoculating loop, glass spreader ("hockey stick"), Bunsen burner
  • Broth culture of a test organism, serial dilution tubes with sterile saline

Procedure

  1. Weigh out nutrient agar according to the manufacturer's instructions and dissolve in distilled water; sterilize by autoclaving at 121°C for 15 minutes.
  2. Pour plates: cool the molten agar to ~45°C, pour into sterile Petri dishes, and allow to solidify.
  3. Streak plate: flame-sterilize a loop, cool it, pick up a loopful of culture, and streak across a quarter of the plate; re-flame the loop, turn the plate, and drag through the previous streaks into a new quadrant — repeat for four quadrants to dilute the inoculum progressively and obtain single, isolated colonies.
  4. Pour-plate count: serially dilute the broth culture in sterile saline; pipette 1 ml of an appropriate dilution into an empty sterile plate, then pour molten agar over it and swirl to mix before it sets.
  5. Spread-plate count: pipette a known volume of diluted culture onto the surface of a set agar plate and spread evenly with a sterile bent-glass rod.
  6. Incubate all plates inverted at 37°C for 18–24 hours, then count colonies and calculate colony-forming units per millilitre (CFU/ml).

Expected Result / Interpretation

The streak plate should yield well-isolated single colonies in the later quadrants, suitable for sub-culturing a pure strain. Pour and spread plates give countable colonies whose number, multiplied by the dilution factor, estimates the original viable cell concentration (CFU/ml).

⚠️ Safety: Allow the autoclave to depressurize fully before opening. Never pour molten agar above ~50°C into a hand-held plate. Flame the loop between each transfer to avoid cross-contamination and aerosol splatter.

MODULE 07

Sterilization and Disinfection

Key definitions · Physical methods · Chemical agents · Efficacy testing

Learning Outcomes

  • Differentiate sterilization, disinfection, antisepsis and sanitization
  • Describe moist heat, dry heat, filtration and radiation methods
  • Classify chemical disinfectants and their mechanisms
  • Describe methods for evaluating antimicrobial efficacy

7.1 Key Definitions

TermMeaning
SterilizationComplete destruction of ALL microbial life, including spores — an absolute term
DisinfectionDestruction of pathogens (not necessarily spores) on inanimate objects
AntisepsisApplying antimicrobial agents to living tissue to reduce microbial load
SanitizationReducing microbes to a publicly safe level (e.g., food-contact surfaces)
DecontaminationMaking an object safe to handle — may fall short of sterilization

7.2 Physical Methods

Autoclaving (121°C, 15 psi, 15–20 min steam under pressure) is the most reliable sterilization method, killing everything including spores by denaturing proteins and nucleic acids — verified with biological indicators (Bacillus stearothermophilus spores) or autoclave tape. Boiling (100°C, 10–15 min) kills vegetative cells but not spores. Pasteurization (flash: 71.7°C/15 sec; batch: 63°C/30 min) kills pathogens without sterilizing, preserving food quality. Tyndallization (100°C/30 min on 3 consecutive days, incubating between) lets spores germinate so the next heating kills them — used for heat-sensitive materials.

Dry heat — a hot-air oven (160–180°C, 2–4 h) sterilizes glassware and metal instruments; incineration and direct flaming destroy waste and sterilize loops instantly. Filtration (0.22/0.45 μm membranes) removes bacteria (not viruses) from heat-sensitive liquids; HEPA filters remove 99.97% of particles ≥0.3 μm from air in laminar flow hoods. Radiation: ionizing (gamma/X-ray) damages DNA directly and sterilizes medical devices without making them radioactive; non-ionizing UV (260 nm) causes thymine dimers but penetrates poorly, so it's used for surface/air disinfection.

7.3 Chemical Agents & Efficacy Testing

ClassExampleNote
PhenolicsLysol, cresolsTuberculocidal, still active with organic matter present
Alcohols70% ethanol/isopropanolFast-evaporating, not sporicidal
HalogensBleach, iodophors (Betadine)Oxidizing agents; iodophors less irritating than free iodine
Oxidizing agentsH₂O₂, peracetic acidPeracetic acid is sporicidal
Quaternary ammonium (Quats)Benzalkonium chlorideGood vs Gram-positives, weaker vs Gram-negatives/spores
AldehydesGlutaraldehyde 2%Cold sterilization of instruments; sporicidal after ~10 h
Ethylene oxide gas—Sterilizes heat-sensitive plastics/electronics; toxic, needs long aeration

Disinfection levels run high (kills all but heavy spore loads — glutaraldehyde, peracetic acid), intermediate (kills vegetative bacteria, mycobacteria, fungi, viruses but not spores — alcohols, iodophors), and low (vegetative bacteria and some viruses/fungi only — quats). Efficacy is measured by the use-dilution test, the disk-diffusion method (zone of inhibition around a soaked disk on a seeded plate), or the classic phenol coefficient (efficacy relative to phenol; >1 means more effective).

🧪 Practical Manual — Evaluating Disinfectant Efficacy (Disk-Diffusion)
Duration: ~2–3 hoursWorks in pairs/small groups

Objective

Compare the antimicrobial effectiveness of different disinfectants/antiseptics using the disk-diffusion technique, and observe the effect of moist vs dry heat sterilization.

Materials

  • Nutrient agar plates freshly seeded (lawn) with a test bacterium
  • Sterile filter-paper disks, forceps, marker pen
  • Test disinfectants (e.g., 70% ethanol, Lysol/phenolic, sodium hypochlorite dilution, povidone-iodine)
  • Access to autoclave and hot-air oven for demonstration
  • Ruler/callipers for measuring zones

Procedure

  1. Using a sterile swab, evenly spread the test culture over the entire surface of a nutrient agar plate to form a confluent lawn.
  2. Using sterile forceps, dip a filter-paper disk in each disinfectant and place it on the seeded agar surface, spacing disks evenly; label each disk's position on the underside of the plate.
  3. Include a disk soaked only in sterile water as a negative control.
  4. Incubate the plate (inverted) at 37°C for 18–24 hours.
  5. Measure the diameter of the clear zone of inhibition around each disk in millimetres and record.
  6. As a separate demonstration, observe how items are loaded into the autoclave and hot-air oven, and note their respective time/temperature settings.

Expected Result / Interpretation

A larger zone of inhibition indicates a more effective disinfectant against the test organism under these conditions. The water control should show no zone. Rank the tested agents from most to least effective based on zone diameter, and relate this back to each agent's mechanism of action.

⚠️ Safety: Handle disinfectants (especially phenolics and hypochlorite) with gloves; avoid mixing bleach with other chemicals. Never open the autoclave until pressure has fully released.

MODULE 08

Microbiology Laboratory

Laboratory layout · Equipment · Reagents · Safety

Learning Outcomes

  • Describe the essential functional areas of a microbiology laboratory
  • Identify major categories of laboratory equipment and their uses
  • List categories of laboratory reagents
  • Explain key laboratory safety practices

8.1 Laboratory Layout

A well-designed microbiology lab is organized into functional zones: a reception/entry area for samples, records and PPE; a media preparation room (balances, pH meters, hot plates, glassware storage); a sterilization area housing autoclaves and hot-air ovens, kept separate due to heat/moisture; the general laboratory/inoculation room with biosafety cabinets, Bunsen burners and microscopes for aseptic work; an incubation room with multiple incubators (37°C for human pathogens, 30°C environmental organisms, 25°C fungi, plus anaerobic jars); a dedicated microscopy room; a biochemical testing area; cold storage (4°C fridges, −20/−80°C freezers) and dry storage; a washing/sterilization area kept apart from clean zones; and a waste disposal area that segregates biohazard, sharps and general waste.

8.2 Equipment

CategoryKey Equipment
SterilizationAutoclave, hot-air oven, Bunsen burner
Culture & isolationInoculating loops/needles, Petri dishes, culture tubes, spreaders
IncubationIncubators (various temperatures), anaerobic jars, CO₂ incubators
MicroscopyCompound light microscope, immersion oil, slides/coverslips
MeasurementAnalytical balance, pH meter, spectrophotometer, pipettes
RefrigerationRefrigerators (4°C), deep freezers (−20/−80°C)
SafetyBiosafety cabinet/laminar flow hood, autoclave for waste, PPE, eyewash station

MODULE 09

Staining Techniques

Simple staining · Gram stain · Acid-fast stain · Capsule & spore stains

Learning Outcomes

  • Explain why staining is necessary for microscopy
  • Perform and interpret the Gram stain
  • Perform and interpret the acid-fast (Ziehl-Neelsen) stain
  • Perform capsule and spore staining and interpret results

9.1 Principles of Staining

Most bacteria are nearly transparent unmodified, so staining increases contrast, reveals morphology, and enables differential identification. Basic stains (crystal violet, methylene blue, safranin) are positively charged and bind the negatively charged bacterial cell; acidic stains (eosin, nigrosin) are negatively charged and are used for negative staining, where the background — not the cell — takes up colour.

9.2 The Gram Stain — the Single Most Important Technique in Microbiology

Based on the peptidoglycan difference between Gram-positive (thick, 60–90% of wall) and Gram-negative (thin, 10–20%, plus an outer membrane) cell walls.

🔬 Gram Stain Procedure
  • Prepare and heat-fix a smear
  • Primary stain: crystal violet, 1 minute — all cells turn purple
  • Rinse; apply Gram's iodine (mordant), 1 minute — forms a crystal violet–iodine complex
  • Rinse; decolorize with 95% ethanol/acetone-alcohol, 10–20 seconds — the critical step
  • Rinse immediately; counterstain with safranin, 30–60 seconds
  • Rinse, blot dry, examine under oil immersion

Gram-positive cells retain the purple complex (thick peptidoglycan traps it) — e.g., Staphylococcus, Streptococcus, Bacillus. Gram-negative cells lose the purple and take up the pink safranin — e.g., E. coli, Salmonella, Pseudomonas. Over-decolorizing makes Gram-positives look negative; under-decolorizing does the reverse — always run known positive (S. aureus) and negative (E. coli) controls.

9.3 Acid-Fast, Capsule & Spore Stains

The Ziehl-Neelsen acid-fast stain identifies Mycobacterium (TB, leprosy) and Nocardia, whose waxy mycolic-acid walls resist decolorization once stained: flood with carbol fuchsin, steam gently 5 minutes, decolorize with acid-alcohol until no more colour runs, counterstain with methylene blue. Acid-fast organisms stay bright red/pink; everything else turns blue.

The capsule stain (negative staining) uses India ink — the capsule excludes the ink and appears as a clear halo around a dark cell on a dark background (no heat fixing, which would destroy the capsule). Important in Streptococcus pneumoniae, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Bacillus anthracis, where the capsule is a major virulence factor resisting phagocytosis.

The Schaeffer-Fulton spore stain uses malachite green steamed onto the smear for 5 minutes (heat drives the dye into the resistant spore coat); after rinsing, vegetative cells decolorize while spores retain the green, then a safranin counterstain turns vegetative cells pink/red. Spore position (terminal, subterminal, central) helps identify Bacillus and Clostridium species.

🧪 Practical Manual — Gram Staining & Special Stains
Duration: ~3 hoursIndividual practical, pairs for controls

Objective

Perform a Gram stain correctly on known and unknown cultures, and observe demonstration slides of acid-fast, capsule and spore stains.

Materials

  • Broth cultures: Staphylococcus aureus (Gram +), Escherichia coli (Gram −), an unknown culture
  • Crystal violet, Gram's iodine, 95% ethanol (or acetone-alcohol), safranin
  • Demonstration slides/reagents for acid-fast (carbol fuchsin, acid-alcohol, methylene blue), capsule (India ink), and spore (malachite green) stains
  • Compound microscope, immersion oil, slides, Bunsen burner

Procedure

  1. Prepare three heat-fixed smears: S. aureus, E. coli, and the unknown.
  2. Flood each with crystal violet for 1 minute; rinse gently with water.
  3. Apply Gram's iodine for 1 minute; rinse.
  4. Decolorize for 10–20 seconds only — stop as soon as the purple stops running freely from the smear; rinse immediately.
  5. Counterstain with safranin for 30–60 seconds; rinse and blot dry (do not rub).
  6. Examine all three slides under oil immersion; record cell colour, shape and arrangement, and use the known controls to judge whether your decolorization step was correct.
  7. Observe the pre-prepared acid-fast, capsule and spore demonstration slides and sketch what you see, noting colour and structure.

Expected Result / Interpretation

S. aureus should appear purple cocci in clusters; E. coli pink/red rods. If your unknown resembles neither control cleanly, your decolorization step (too long or too short) is the most likely source of error — repeat with a fresh smear. On demonstration slides: acid-fast bacilli appear bright red/pink against a blue background; encapsulated cells show a clear halo against a dark India-ink background; spores appear green within pink/red vegetative cells.

⚠️ Safety: Ethanol and acetone-alcohol are flammable — keep away from the open Bunsen flame while decolorizing. Acid-alcohol and carbol fuchsin are irritants; wear gloves and avoid skin/eye contact.

Antigens and Antibodies

Antigen/antibody basics · Immunoglobulin classes · Serological tests · Primary vs secondary response

Learning Outcomes

  • Define antigen and antibody and describe antibody structure
  • List and distinguish the five immunoglobulin classes
  • Describe agglutination, precipitation and ELISA-based tests
  • Differentiate the primary and secondary immune response

10.1 Antibody Structure & Immunoglobulin Classes

An antibody (immunoglobulin) is a Y-shaped protein produced by B cells/plasma cells that binds a specific antigen. Each monomer has two heavy chains and two light chains linked by disulfide bonds, forming two antigen-binding Fab regions and one Fc region that interacts with immune effector cells.

ClassKey Feature
IgMFirst antibody made in a primary response; pentamer; efficient at agglutination
IgGMost abundant in blood; crosses the placenta; dominates the secondary response
IgAFound in secretions — saliva, tears, mucus, breast milk
IgEMediates allergic reactions and anti-parasite defence
IgDMainly a B-cell surface receptor; role still being clarified

10.2 Serological Tests

🧫 Common Test Formats
  • Agglutination — antibody clumps particulate antigen (direct: blood typing, bacterial ID; indirect/passive: latex beads or RBCs carrying antigen)
  • Precipitation — soluble antigen + antibody form a visible precipitate (ring test, Ouchterlony double immunodiffusion)
  • ELISA — enzyme-labelled antibody/antigen gives a colour change; direct, indirect (patient antibody detection — most common), sandwich (very specific antigen detection), or competitive
  • Western blot — electrophoresis-separated proteins probed with antibody; used to confirm HIV
  • Immunofluorescence — fluorescent-labelled antibody visualized by fluorescence microscopy (direct/indirect)

In a primary response, IgM appears first (days 5–10) followed by IgG (days 10–14) — slower and weaker. On re-exposure, the secondary (anamnestic) response is faster and stronger, dominated by IgG due to memory cells. Diagnostically: IgM signals a recent/acute infection, IgG signals past infection or immunity, and a four-fold rise in IgG titre between paired sera indicates active infection.

🧪 Practical Manual — ABO/Rh Blood Typing by Slide Agglutination
Duration: ~2 hoursIndividual or pairs; uses simulated/donor blood samples

Objective

Determine ABO and Rh blood group using the direct slide agglutination test, illustrating antigen-antibody clumping reactions.

Materials

  • Anti-A, Anti-B and Anti-D (Rh) typing sera (or simulated blood-typing kit)
  • Blood sample (finger-prick, or simulated/synthetic sample per institutional policy)
  • Glass slide or typing tile, sterile lancets, applicator sticks, cotton wool, alcohol swabs

Procedure

  1. Divide a clean glass slide/tile into three labelled sections: Anti-A, Anti-B, Anti-D.
  2. Place one drop of blood into each section.
  3. Add one drop of the corresponding antiserum to each section (Anti-A, Anti-B, Anti-D respectively).
  4. Mix each with a clean applicator stick (a different stick for each section) and gently rock the slide for up to 2 minutes.
  5. Observe each section for clumping (agglutination) versus a smooth, uniform suspension.
  6. Record which sera caused agglutination and deduce the blood group accordingly.

Expected Result / Interpretation

Agglutination with Anti-A only = Group A; Anti-B only = Group B; both = Group AB; neither = Group O. Agglutination with Anti-D indicates Rh-positive; no agglutination indicates Rh-negative. Clumping is a direct visual demonstration of antibody cross-linking particulate (red cell surface) antigen.

⚠️ Safety: Treat all blood samples as potentially infectious — use a fresh sterile lancet per person, wear gloves, and dispose of lancets in a sharps container. Follow institutional biosafety policy; simulated blood-typing kits are recommended where available.

Microbial Genetics and Variation

Central dogma · Mutations · Transformation, transduction, conjugation · Antibiotic resistance

Learning Outcomes

  • Describe the central dogma and DNA replication in bacteria
  • Classify types and causes of mutation
  • Differentiate transformation, transduction and conjugation
  • Explain mechanisms and spread of antibiotic resistance

11.1 Mutations

A mutation is a permanent, heritable change in the DNA sequence. Types include point mutations (a single base substitution — silent, missense, or nonsense), insertions/deletions (which can cause a frameshift), and larger chromosomal rearrangements. Causes are spontaneous (replication errors) or induced by mutagens — chemicals, UV light (forming thymine dimers), or ionizing radiation. Cells counter damage with DNA repair mechanisms such as photoreactivation, excision repair, and mismatch repair.

11.2 Genetic Transfer in Bacteria

MechanismDNA SourceKey Feature
TransformationFree DNA from the environmentRecipient must be "competent" to take up naked DNA
TransductionDNA packaged in a bacteriophageA phage accidentally carries bacterial DNA to a new host
ConjugationDirect cell-to-cell transferRequires physical contact via a sex pilus (F⁺ → F⁻)

These horizontal gene transfer mechanisms are central to spreading antibiotic resistance genes, often carried on plasmids or transposons. Resistance mechanisms include enzymatic drug inactivation, modification of the drug target, decreased drug uptake, and efflux pumps that actively expel the antibiotic.

🧪 Practical Manual — Effect of UV Radiation on Bacterial Survival
Duration: ~2 hours (plus overnight incubation)Works in pairs/small groups

Objective

Demonstrate the mutagenic/lethal effect of UV radiation on bacteria by exposing part of a seeded plate to UV light and comparing colony survival with an unexposed control area.

Materials

  • Nutrient agar plate freshly seeded with a bacterial suspension (spread plate)
  • UV lamp (germicidal, in a biosafety cabinet or UV box), opaque card/foil mask
  • Sterile swab, incubator

Procedure

  1. Spread-plate a dilute bacterial suspension evenly onto a nutrient agar plate.
  2. Cover half the plate with an opaque card or foil (the shielded control half); leave the other half exposed.
  3. With the lid removed, expose the plate to UV light for a set time (e.g., 30–60 seconds) at a fixed distance from the lamp.
  4. Replace the lid, wrap the plate to exclude further light (to reduce photoreactivation repair), and incubate at 37°C for 18–24 hours.
  5. Compare colony growth on the exposed versus shielded half.

Expected Result / Interpretation

The shielded half should show normal, dense colony growth, while the UV-exposed half shows markedly reduced colony numbers (or none), demonstrating UV-induced DNA damage (thymine dimer formation) that is lethal or mutagenic to unprotected cells. Any surviving colonies on the exposed side may represent UV-resistant mutants or cells that underwent successful DNA repair.

⚠️ Safety: Never look directly at a UV lamp or expose bare skin to germicidal UV — it damages eyes and skin. Perform exposure inside a UV cabinet with the safety interlock, or wear UV-protective eyewear and gloves if using an open lamp.

MODULE 12

Environmental Microbiology

Water · Soil · Air · Food & dairy microbiology

Learning Outcomes

  • Explain indicator organisms and water quality testing
  • Describe the roles of soil microorganisms in nutrient cycling
  • Describe the sources and behaviour of airborne microbes
  • Describe food/dairy spoilage, preservation and quality testing

12.1 Water Microbiology

E. coli and other coliforms serve as indicator organisms for faecal contamination — easy to detect, normally present in the gut, and their presence signals a risk of enteric pathogens even when the pathogens themselves aren't directly tested for. Water quality is assessed by the Most Probable Number (MPN) method (a statistical estimate from multiple-tube fermentation) or by membrane filtration, where a known water volume is filtered, the membrane placed on selective medium, and coliform colonies counted directly. Municipal wastewater treatment proceeds through preliminary screening, primary (settling), secondary (biological/activated sludge), and sometimes tertiary treatment (e.g., chlorination) before discharge or reuse.

12.2 Soil & Air Microbiology

Soil teems with life: bacteria (10⁶–10⁹ per gram) are the most numerous — decomposers, nitrogen fixers, nitrifiers; fungi decompose cellulose/lignin and form mycorrhizae; filamentous actinomycetes decompose complex organic matter, produce antibiotics, and give soil its earthy smell (geosmin). Soil bacteria drive the nitrogen cycle: Rhizobium (symbiotic) and Azotobacter/Clostridium (free-living) fix N₂; Nitrosomonas then Nitrobacter nitrify ammonia to nitrite to nitrate; denitrifiers return N₂ to the atmosphere.

Air is not a natural habitat for microbes — they are only transiently present, carried in as soil dust, respiratory droplets, or water aerosols. Outdoor air typically carries 100–1000 microbes/m³. Bacterial and fungal spores survive airborne transit well; vegetative bacteria die quickly from desiccation and UV. Droplet transmission (large particles, >5 μm, short range) spreads diseases like influenza, while smaller droplet nuclei can travel much further.

12.3 Food & Dairy Microbiology

Food quality/safety testing includes Standard Plate Count (total aerobic bacteria — Grade A pasteurized milk should read <20,000 CFU/ml), coliform count (sanitation indicator, <10 CFU/ml in pasteurized milk), and the phosphatase test (this milk enzyme is destroyed by proper pasteurization, so a negative result confirms adequate heat treatment). Preservation methods include pasteurization, refrigeration/freezing, drying, salting/sugaring (osmotic stress), and fermentation. Beneficial fermentations (yoghurt, cheese via lactic acid bacteria) are the flip side of the same microbial activity that, uncontrolled, causes spoilage and foodborne illness (Salmonella, Listeria, Campylobacter).

🧪 Practical Manual — Water Quality Testing (Most Probable Number Coliform Test)
Duration: ~1.5 hours setup + 24–48 h incubationWorks in small groups

Objective

Estimate the coliform content of a water sample using the multiple-tube (MPN) fermentation technique as an indicator of faecal contamination.

Materials

  • Water sample (e.g., tap water, well water, or a stream sample)
  • Lactose broth (or MacConkey broth) with inverted Durham tubes, in triplicate sets across a dilution series
  • Sterile pipettes, test tube rack, incubator, MPN statistical table

Procedure

  1. Prepare three sets of five (or three) lactose broth tubes with Durham tubes, for three decimal dilutions of the water sample (e.g., 10 ml, 1 ml, 0.1 ml volumes).
  2. Inoculate each tube with the corresponding sample volume/dilution.
  3. Incubate all tubes at 35–37°C for 24–48 hours.
  4. Examine each tube for gas production (bubble in the Durham tube) and turbidity/acid production, indicating a positive (presumptive coliform) result.
  5. Record the number of positive tubes at each dilution as a three-digit code and consult a standard MPN table to estimate the Most Probable Number of coliforms per 100 ml.

Expected Result / Interpretation

Gas and acid production in a tube is a presumptive positive for coliforms. The pattern of positive tubes across the dilution series, read against the MPN table, gives a statistical estimate of coliform density — higher MPN values indicate greater faecal contamination and higher risk of waterborne pathogens.

⚠️ Safety: Treat all water samples, especially environmental ones, as potentially contaminated. Autoclave all used tubes before washing/disposal.

13.1 Mechanisms of Antimicrobial Action

MechanismExample DrugsTarget
Cell wall synthesis inhibitionPenicillins, cephalosporins, vancomycinPeptidoglycan cross-linking
Protein synthesis inhibitionTetracyclines, aminoglycosides, macrolidesBacterial (70S) ribosome
Nucleic acid synthesis inhibitionFluoroquinolones, rifampicinDNA gyrase / RNA polymerase

An ideal antimicrobial agent shows selective toxicity (harms the microbe, not the host), a narrow enough spectrum to spare normal flora where possible, minimal side effects, and low potential to induce resistance.

13.2 Antibiotic Sensitivity Testing — Kirby-Bauer Disc Diffusion

Disc diffusion determines which antibiotics are effective against a specific isolate, guiding therapy and flagging resistance. An antibiotic diffuses out from a paper disc into the agar, forming a concentration gradient; bacteria are inhibited within a zone of inhibition whose diameter reflects susceptibility.

🧫 Kirby-Bauer Procedure
  • Adjust the pure culture to a 0.5 McFarland standard (~1.5 × 10⁸ CFU/ml)
  • Swab evenly across the entire surface of a Mueller-Hinton agar plate
  • Apply 6–12 antibiotic discs with sterile forceps, spaced apart, pressing firmly
  • Incubate at 35–37°C for 16–18 hours, aerobically
  • Measure each zone of inhibition diameter in millimetres
  • Interpret against CLSI charts as Susceptible (S), Intermediate (I), or Resistant (R)

Disc diffusion is simple, inexpensive and tests multiple antibiotics at once, but is only qualitative. MIC (Minimum Inhibitory Concentration) — the lowest antibiotic concentration that prevents visible growth — is obtained quantitatively by broth dilution or the E-test (a gradient strip read where the elliptical inhibition zone crosses it), and automated systems (VITEK, MicroScan) combine identification with susceptibility testing.

🧪 Practical Manual — Antibiotic Sensitivity Testing (Kirby-Bauer Disc Diffusion)
Duration: ~2 hours setup + 16–18 h incubationWorks in pairs/small groups

Objective

Determine the antibiotic susceptibility profile of a bacterial isolate using the standardized Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion method.

Materials

  • Pure broth culture of a test organism, sterile saline, McFarland 0.5 turbidity standard
  • Mueller-Hinton agar plates, sterile cotton swabs
  • Antibiotic discs (e.g., ampicillin, gentamicin, ciprofloxacin, tetracycline, chloramphenicol), sterile forceps
  • Incubator, ruler or callipers, CLSI interpretive chart

Procedure

  1. Prepare a bacterial suspension in sterile saline and adjust its turbidity to match the 0.5 McFarland standard.
  2. Dip a sterile swab into the suspension, express excess liquid against the tube wall, and streak it evenly over the entire Mueller-Hinton agar surface in three directions to form a confluent lawn; let it dry for a few minutes.
  3. Using sterile forceps, place each antibiotic disc onto the agar surface, spacing discs at least 24 mm apart (centre to centre) and pressing gently so each disc makes full contact.
  4. Invert and incubate the plate at 35–37°C for 16–18 hours.
  5. Measure the diameter of the clear zone of inhibition around each disc to the nearest millimetre.
  6. Compare each measurement against the CLSI interpretive chart for that antibiotic/organism combination and classify as Susceptible, Intermediate, or Resistant.

Expected Result / Interpretation

A larger zone generally indicates greater susceptibility. No zone (growth right up to the disc) indicates resistance. The final susceptibility report — a list of S/I/R calls per antibiotic tested — is exactly the kind of result a clinical microbiology lab would send back to a physician to guide antibiotic choice.

⚠️ Safety: Work near an open flame or in a biosafety cabinet when swabbing to reduce aerosol risk. Dispose of used swabs and plates by autoclaving before discard, and never re-use antibiotic discs between plates.
